\subsection{Properties of the density matrix functional.}

\textbf{$\mathcal{F}[\m{D}(r)]$ is an analytic matrix functional.}
Since $\m{D}(\vec{r})$ is the projection of $\hat{\rho}(\vec{r})$ into the subspace $\mathbb{V}^{N}$, while ${\mathcal{H}[\m{D}(\vec{r})] = \mathcal{F}[\m{D}(\vec{r})] + \int d^3r ~ v(\vec{r}) \m{D}(\vec{r})}$ is the projection of the Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$, the matrix density functional has to transform under an arbitrary basis transformation $\m{L} : \mathbb{C}^{N \times N} \to \mathbb{C}^{N \times N}$ as \cite{Lu2022JPCL}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}[\m{L} \m{D}(\vec{r}) \m{L}^{-1}] = \m{L} \mathcal{F}[\mathbf{D}(\vec{r})] \mathbf{L}^{-1} \label{eqn:basis_transformation_symmetry}
\end{equation}
This transformation property implies that $\mathcal{F}$ is an \emph{analytic matrix functional}. As we know, an analytic function of a scalar variable, $f(z): \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$ is defined by its Taylor series around some point $z_0$:
\begin{equation}
f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}}{n!} (z-z_0)^n
\end{equation}
With the help of the Taylor series the scalar function can be turned into a analytic matrix function, $f(\m{Z}): \mathbb{C}^{N \times N} \to \mathbb{C}^{N \times N}$, where the product of complex numbers is replaced by the matrix product
\begin{equation}
f(\m{Z}) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}}{n!} (\m{Z} - z_0 \m{I})^n
\end{equation}
where $\m{I}$ is the identity matrix. If the Taylor series converges everywhere, we can take $z_0 = 0$ without loss of generality.
The coefficients $f^{(n)}$ are independent of the matrix dimension. Under a similarity transformation, an analytic matrix function behaves as
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  f(\m{L} \m{Z} \m{L}^{-1}) &= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}}{n!} (\m{L} \m{Z} \m{L}^{-1})^n \\
  &= f^{(0)} \underbrace{\m{L} \m{L}^{-1}}_{\m{I}} \m{I}
  + f^{(1)} \m{L} \m{Z} \m{L}^{-1}
  + \frac{f^{(2)}}{2} \m{L} \m{Z} \underbrace{\m{L}^{-1} \m{L}}_{\m{I}} \m{Z} \m{L}^{-1}
  + \ldots
  + \frac{f^{(n)}}{n!} \m{L} \m{Z}^n \m{L}^{-1} + \ldots \\
  &= \m{L} f(\m{Z}) \m{L}^{-1}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The definitions of analytic matrix functions in terms of a Taylor series or the transformation property are equivalent. \cite{Rinehart1955} 
An analytical matrix \emph{functional} $\mathcal{F}[\m{D}(\vec{r})] : (L^1)^{N \times N} \to \mathbb{C}^{N \times N}$ takes a matrix of $L^1$-integrable functions, which depend on the 3-dimensional spatial coordinate vector $\vec{r}$, as an argument.
If we interpret $\mathcal{F}$ as a general functional of $N^2$ functions, its functional Taylor expansion around $\m{D}(r) = 0$ would be
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    \mathcal{F}[\m{D}(\vec{r})] & = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n!} \int d^3\!r_1 d^3\!r_2 \cdots d^3\!r_n ~ \\
    & \quad \sum_{A,B,C,D,\ldots}^{N} \frac{\delta^{n} \mathcal{F}[\m{D}(\vec{r})]}{\delta D_{AB}(\vec{r}_1) \delta D_{CD}(\vec{r}_2) \ldots \delta D_{YZ}(\vec{r}_n)} D_{AB}(\vec{r}_1) D_{CD}(\vec{r}_2) \ldots D_{YZ}(\vec{r}_n).
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
where the Taylor coefficients are themselves $N^{2 n}$-dimensional tensors of functionals of the spatial coordinates $\vec{r}_1,\ldots,\vec{r}_n$.
However, because of the transformation property of Eqn.~\ref{eqn:basis_transformation_symmetry} the Taylor expansion of the matrix density functional
$\mathcal{F}[\m{D}(\vec{r})]$ is constrained to have the form
\begin{equation}
 \begin{split}
   \mathcal{F}[\m{D}(\vec{r})] &= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n!} \int d^3\!r_1 d^3\!r_2 \cdots d^3\!r_n ~ \\
   & \quad f^{(n)}(\vec{r}_1,\ldots,\vec{r}_n; \mathcal{N}(\vec{r}), \tr(\m{D}(\vec{r})), \ldots) \m{D}(\vec{r}_1) \m{D}(\vec{r}_2) \cdots \m{D}(\vec{r}_n) \label{eqn:functional_taylor_expansion}
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
The coefficients $f^{(n)}$ are scalar functions and the matrix density occurs either as a matrix product or in scalar quantities that are invariant under basis transformations, such as the trace $\tr(\mathbf{D}(\vec{r}))$. The translation property does not exclude the possibility that the functional depends explicitly on the dimension of the subspace, which can be expressed as $\mathcal{N}(\vec{r}) = \tr\{\m{D}(\vec{r}) \m{D}^{-1}(\vec{r})\}$ and therefore is also a functional of the matrix density.
The following expressions, for instance, are formally valid analytical matrix functionals
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{G}_{AC}[\m{D}(\vec{r})] &= \int d^3\!r_1 \int d^3\!r_2 \frac{\sum_{B=1}^{N} D_{AB}(\vec{r}_1) D_{BC}(\vec{r}_2)}{\vert \vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2 \vert} \\
  \mathcal{G}_{AB}[\m{D}(\vec{r})] &= \int d^3\!r \nabla \rho_{V}(\vec{r}) \cdot \nabla D_{AB}(\vec{r})
\end{align}
because they satisfy the transformation property of Eqn.~\ref{eqn:basis_transformation_symmetry}. On the other hand, expressions like
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{G}_{AB}[\m{D}(\vec{r})] = \int d^3\!r_1 \int d^3\!r_2 \frac{D_{AA}(\vec{r}_1) D_{BB}(\vec{r}_2)}{\vert \vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2 \vert}
\end{equation}
are not analytical, because they do not transform correctly under basis transformations.

\textbf{$\mathcal{F}[\m{D}(\vec{r})]$ does not depend on the subspace dimension.}
Although an analytical matrix functional can accept a matrix density of any dimension, it is not immediately obvious that the functional has to be the same independently of the dimension of the subspace. The ensemble density functional theory of Gross, Oliveira and Kohn \cite{Gross1988ReyleighRitz,Gross1988EnsembleDFT} certainly depends on the weights of the states, so that there is a different functional for an ensemble of 2,3,4,... states. It is conceivable that a similar dependence on the subspace dimension exists for the matrix density functional $\mathcal{F}[\m{D}(\vec{r})]$. However, the following considerations (which falls short of a rigorous proof) suggests that this is not the case and that the same matrix functional applies to all subspace dimensions:
Let us suppose that the matrix functional could be different and denote the functional for a subspace with dimension $N$ as $\mathcal{F}^{(N)}$.
Let $v(r)$ be the external potential (for instance the Coulomb attraction to the nuclei in a molecule) that is localized around the origin $\vec{O}$. $v(r)$ decays lik $\propto -\frac{1}{r}$ or faster with distance. A copy of the same nuclei is put at a distant point $\vec{R}$, so that the total external potential is $v(\vec{r}) + v(\vec{r}-\vec{R})$ as shown in Fig.\ref{fig:repeated_effective_potential}

\begin{figure}[h!]
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/repeated_effective_potential.png}
  \caption{\label{fig:repeated_effective_potential} Effective potential of two identical molecules separated by a large distance $R$. The localized wavefunction in the left and right potential wells are identical except for the rigid shift.}
\end{figure}

$\mathbb{V}_{\vec{O}}^N$ is the subspace spanned by $N$ electronic wavefunctions (not necessarily eigenstates) that are localized around the origin $\vec{O}$. These wavefunctions decay exponentially with distance. $\mathbb{V}_{\vec{R}}^N$ is spanned by the same wavefunctions rigidly translated to the center $\vec{R}$. A subspace of twice the dimension is formed by combining the subspaces of the left and right molecules,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    \mathbb{V}^{2N} = \mathbb{V}^{N}_{\vec{O}} \cup \mathbb{V}^{N}_{\vec{R}} = \Span\Big\{ &
                  \Psi_1(\vec{r}_1,\vec{r}_2,\ldots),
                  \Psi_2(\vec{r}_1,\vec{r}_2,\ldots), \ldots,
                  \Psi_N(\vec{r}_1,\vec{r}_2,\ldots), \\
    &             \Psi_1(\vec{r}_1-\vec{R},\vec{r}_2-\vec{R},\ldots),
                  \Psi_2(\vec{r}_1-\vec{R},\vec{r}_2-\vec{R},\ldots), \ldots,
                  \Psi_N(\vec{r}_1-\vec{R},\vec{r}_2-\vec{R},\ldots) \Big\}.
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
Because of the large distance $R$, the transition densities between states localized at $\vec{O}$ and those at $\vec{R}$ vanishes. The state densities and transition densities among the states localized on the same molecule are the same for both moleules except for a rigid shift. The matrix density for the combined subspace therefore consists of two blocks of size $N$, each:
\begin{equation}
  \m{D}^{(2N)}(\vec{r}) =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}) & 0 \\
    0 & \m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}-\vec{R})
  \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
As the distance between the two molecules is increased ($R \to \infty$), the matrix elements of the Hamiltonian vanishes, $\langle \Psi_{A} \vert \hat{H} \vert \Psi_{B} \rangle \to 0$, if the wavefunctions $\Psi_A$ and $\Psi_B$ are localized on different molecules. The matrix elements of the kinetic energy decrease exponentially, $\langle \Psi_{A} \vert \hat{T} \vert \Psi_{B} \rangle \propto \exp(-\alpha R)$, for the Coulomb operator the decrease is like $\langle \Psi_{A} \vert \hat{W} \vert \Psi_{B} \rangle \propto \frac{1}{R}$. Therefore, in the limit $R \to \infty$, the universal part of the Hamiltonian in the subspace $\mathbb{V}^{(2 N)}$ is just block-diagonal, $\hat{H}^{(2N)}_0 = \hat{H}^{(N)} \oplus \hat{H}^{(N)}$. The projection of $\hat{H}_0$ into the combined subspace $\mathbb{V}^{(2N)}$ is a functional of the matrix density $\m{D}^{(2N)}$, and the Hamiltonians in the individual subspaces $\mathbb{V}^{(N)}_{\vec{O}}$ and $\mathbb{V}^{(N)}_{\vec{R}}$ are functionals of the matrix densities $\m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r})$ and $\m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}-\vec{R})$, respectively. Furthermore the universal functional should be translationally invariant, so that $\mathcal{F}^{(N)}[\m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}-\vec{R})] = \mathcal{F}^{(N)}[\m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r})] = \m{H}^{(N)}_0$. Therefore the following relation holds between the functionals $\mathcal{F}^{(2N)}$ and $\mathcal{F}^{(N)}$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{F}^{(2N)}
  \left[
    \begin{pmatrix}
      \m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}) & 0 \\
      0 & \m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}-\vec{R})
    \end{pmatrix}
    \right]
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \m{H}^{(N)}_{0} & 0 \\
    0 & \m{H}^{(N)}_{0}
  \end{pmatrix}
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \mathcal{F}^{(N)}[\m{D}(\vec{r})] & 0 \\
    0 & \mathcal{F}^{(N)}[\m{D}(\vec{r})]
  \end{pmatrix} \label{eqn:relation_F2N_FN}
\end{equation}

The plan is to plug the functional Taylor expansion of Eqn.~\ref{eqn:functional_taylor_expansion} into Eqn.~\ref{eqn:relation_F2N_FN} in order to work out the relation between the functional $\mathcal{F}^{(2N)}$ and $\mathcal{F}^{(N)}$. A priori it is assumed that the functional could be different for different subspace dimensions. It will be shown that this is not the case. However, Eqn.~\ref{eqn:relation_F2N_FN} is not yet in a suitable form to compare the two functionals, since on its LHS the functional $\mathcal{F}^{(2N)}$ operates on a matrix density of dimension $2N$, while on the RHS the arguments of the functional $\mathcal{F}^{(N)}$ have dimension $N$. So we have to transform the expession in such a way, that $\mathcal{F}^{(2N)}$ operates on a matrix density of dimension $N$.
%%%

First some preliminaries.
The functional Taylor expansion involves integrations over three-dimensional space.
The three-dimensional space is partitioned into Voronoi polyhedra. Each point $\vec{r}$ is assigned to the closest molecule,
\begin{align}
  V_{\vec{O}} &= \{\vec{r}: \vert \vec{r} \vert < \vert \vec{r} - \vec{R} \vert \} \\
  V_{\vec{R}} &= \{\vec{r}: \vert \vec{r} - \vec{R} \vert \leq \vert \vec{r} \vert \} \\
  \mathbb{R}^3 &= V_{\vec{O}} \cup V_{\vec{R}}.
\end{align}
Integration over space is split into separate integrations over the Voronoi polyhedra,
\begin{equation}
 \int_{\mathbb{R}^3} \ldots d^3\!r = \int_{V_{\vec{O}}} \ldots d^3\!r + \int_{V_{\vec{R}}} \ldots d^3\!r.
\end{equation}
Since the wavefunctions are localized around the potential at either $\vec{O}$ or $\vec{R}$, there is no point in space where $\m{D}(\vec{r})$ and $\m{D}(\vec{r}-\vec{R})$ are simultaneously non-zero. So dependening on where the point $\vec{r}$ lies, the projection of the density operator in the subspace $\mathbb{V}^{2N}$ has only one non-zero block at the top or at the bottom:
\begin{equation}
  \m{D}^{(2N)}(\vec{r}) = \m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}) \oplus \m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}-\vec{R}) =
  \begin{cases}
    \m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}) \oplus 0 & \text{if } \vec{r} \in V_{\vec{O}} \\
    0 \oplus \m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}-\vec{R}) & \text{if } \vec{r} \in V_{\vec{R}} \label{eqn:D_split}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Similarly we get for the trace,
\begin{equation}
  \tr\left(\m{D}^{(2N)}(\vec{r}) \right)
  =
  \tr\left(\m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}) \oplus \m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}-\vec{R}) \right)
  =
  \begin{cases}
    \tr(\m{D}(\vec{r})) & \text{if } \vec{r} \in V_{\vec{O}} \\
    \tr(\m{D}(\vec{r}-\vec{R}) & \text{if } \vec{r} \in V_{\vec{R}} \label{eqn:tr_split}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
If the functional should depend explicitly on the subspace dimension, the number of electronic states has to be expressed as a functional of the matrix density. One way to do this is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{N}(\vec{r}) = \tr\left\{ \m{D}(\vec{r}) \m{D}^{-1}(\vec{r}) \right\}.
\end{equation}
If at some point the matrix density does not have full rank and therefore is not invertible, the pseudinverse is used instead of the inverse. $\mathcal{N}(\vec{r})$ always equals the rank of the matrix density.
Since $\m{D}^{(2N)}(\vec{r})$ has at most rank $N$ (the number of wavefunctions that are simultaneously non-zero is at most $N$),
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{N}^{(2N)}(\vec{r})
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \mathcal{N}^{(N)}(\vec{r}) & \text{if } \vec{r} \in V_{\vec{O}} \\
    \mathcal{N}^{(N)}(\vec{r}-\vec{R}) & \text{if } \vec{r} \in V_{\vec{R}}. \label{eqn:N_split}
  \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

Now we right out the Taylor expansion and focus only on the term linear in $\m{D}^{(2N)}(\vec{r})$, but higher-order terms work the same way:

\begin{align}
  &\mathcal{F}^{(2N)}[\m{D}^{(2N)}(\vec{r})] = \int d^3\!r_1 ~ f^{(1)}\left(\vec{r}_1; \tr(\m{D}^{(2N)}(\vec{r}_1)), \mathcal{N}^{(2N)}(\vec{r}_1) \right) \m{D}^{(2N)}(\vec{r}_1) + \ldots \\
    =& \int d^3\!r_1 ~ f^{(1)}\left(\vec{r}_1; \tr(\m{D}^{(2N)}(\vec{r}_1)), \mathcal{N}^{(2N)}(\vec{r}_1) \right)
    \begin{pmatrix}
      \m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}_1) & 0 \\
      0 & \m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}_1 - \vec{R})
    \end{pmatrix} + \ldots \\
    =& \int_{V_{\vec{O}}} d^3\!r_1 ~ f^{(1)}\left(\vec{r}_1; \tr(\m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}_1)), \mathcal{N}^{(N)}(\vec{r}_1) \right)
    \begin{pmatrix}
      \m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}_1) & 0 \\
      0 & 0
    \end{pmatrix} \\
    & + \int_{V_{\vec{R}}} d^3\!r_1 ~ f^{(1)}\left(\vec{r}_1; \tr(\m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}_1-\vec{R})), \mathcal{N}^{(N)}(\vec{r}_1-\vec{R}) \right)
    \begin{pmatrix}
      0 & 0 \\
      0 & \m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}_1-\vec{R})
    \end{pmatrix} + \ldots \\
    =&
    \begin{pmatrix}
      \int_{\mathbb{R}^3} d^3\!r_1 ~ f^{(1)}\left(\vec{r}_1; \tr(\m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}_1)), \mathcal{N}^{(N)}(\vec{r}_1) \right) \m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}_1) + \ldots & 0 \\
      0 & \int_{\mathbb{R}^3} d^3\!r_1 ~ f^{(1)}\left(\ldots \right) \m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}_1-\vec{R}) + \ldots\\
    \end{pmatrix} \\
    =&
    \begin{pmatrix}
      \mathcal{F}^{(2N)}[\m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r})] & 0 \\
      0 & \mathcal{F}^{(2N)}[\m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r})]
    \end{pmatrix} \label{eqn:relation_F2N_FN_same_dim}
\end{align}

In going from line 2 to 3, the integration was split up and the corresponding cases from Eqns.~\ref{eqn:D_split},\ref{eqn:tr_split},\ref{eqn:N_split} were used inside each integration volume. In going from lines 3 and 4 to line 5, the integration range was extended again over the entire $\mathbb{R}^3$, since the respective integrands vanish on the additional volume. Finally, to get to the last line, we sum up the Taylor expansion again to obtain $\mathcal{F}^{(2N)}$ evaluated on the matrix density in the subspaces $\mathbb{V}_{\vec{O}}$ and $\mathbb{V}_{\vec{R}}$, respectively. As the universal functional has translational invariance, we can use $\mathcal{F}^{(2N)}[\m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r}-\vec{R})] = \mathcal{F}^{(2N)}[\m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r})]$.
Comparison of Eqn.~\ref{eqn:relation_F2N_FN_same_dim} with Eqn.~\ref{eqn:relation_F2N_FN} shows that
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{F}^{(2N)}[\m{D}^{N}(\vec{r})] = \mathcal{F}^{(N)}[\m{D}^{N}(\vec{r})]
\end{equation}
for any matrix density $\m{D}^{(N)}(\vec{r})$ that decays exponentially with distance.
Now the dimension of the argument is the same on both sides. The above equality suggests that the same universal functional applies to all subspace dimensions and one can drop the superscript writing simply $\mathcal{F}[\m{D}(\vec{r})]$. For $N=1$ it is equal to the ground-state kinetic and Hartree-exchange-correlation (Hxc) functionals,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{F}[\rho(\vec{r})] = T[\rho(\vec{r})] + E_{\text{Hxc}}[\rho(\vec{r})]
\end{equation}
However, deducing the multi-state functional from known ground-state kinetic-energy and Hartree-exchange-correlation functionals is not possible. Even if one has a closed expression of the functionals in term of $\rho(\vec{r})$, $\nabla \rho(\vec{r})$, $\ldots$, it is not clear where $\rho(\vec{r})$ has to be replaced by the scalar $\tr(\m{D}(\vec{r}))$ and where by the matrix $\m{D}(\vec{r})$. For $N=1$, all three are the same, i.e. $\rho(\vec{r}) = \tr(\m{D}(\vec{r})) = \m{D}(\vec{r})$.
Nevertheless ground-state functionals can give some guidance.

The next step is to find a multistate matrix density functional, that satisfies the above constraints and ideally reduces to some known ground state functional for $N=1$.
\subsection{Kinetic energy functional}
\textbf{Multistate von-Weizs\"{a}cker kinetic functional I (vW1e). \label{sec:vW1e}} We start with the von-Weizs\"{a}cker kinetic energy functional, which can be extended to multiple electronic states and is exact for one-electron systems.
In the following $A,B,\ldots,I,J,K,L = 1,\ldots,N$ enumerate electronic states, $\alpha,\beta=x,y,z$ denote Cartesian coordinates and partial derivatives with respect to Cartesian coordinates are abbreviated as $\partial_{\alpha} f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial_{r_\alpha}}$. 
Let the single particle wavefunctions be denoted as $\phi_A(\vec{r})$. The matrix elements of the kinetic energy operator are
\begin{equation}
T_{A,B} = -\frac{1}{2} \int d^3\!r ~ \phi^*_A(\vec{r}) \nabla^2 \phi_B(\vec{r}).
\end{equation}
Plugging the vector field $\vec{F}(\vec{r}) = \phi^*_A \nabla \phi_B$ into Gauss' law,
\begin{equation}
 \int_V (\nabla \cdot \vec{F}) ~ d^3\!r = \int_{\partial V} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{S},
\end{equation}
and noting that the surface integral on the right hand side disappears as the volume $V \to \mathbb{R}^3$, because $\vec{F}(\vec{r})$ decays exponentially,
one finds that
\begin{equation}
 \int_{\mathbb{R}^3} d^3\!r ~ \nabla \cdot \vec{F} = \int d^3\!r \sum_{\alpha=x,y,z} \left\{ (\partial_{\alpha} \phi^*_A)(\partial_{\alpha} \phi_B) + \phi^*_A \partial^2_{\alpha} \phi_B  \right\} = 0,
\end{equation}
or after rearrangeing
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    T_{A,B} &= -\frac{1}{2} \int d^3\!r ~ \phi^*_A \nabla^2 \phi_B \\
    &= -\frac{1}{2} \int d^3\!r ~ \sum_{\alpha} \phi^*_A \partial^2_{\alpha} \phi_B = \frac{1}{2} \int d^3\!r ~ \sum_{\alpha} (\partial_{\alpha} \phi^*_A)(\partial_{\alpha} \phi_B) \\
    &= \int d^3\!r ~ \frac{1}{2} (\nabla \phi^*_A)\cdot (\nabla \phi_B) \label{eqn:kinetic_energy_1e}.
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
For a single-electron system, the matrix density is just the product of the wavefunctions (the density matrix),
\begin{equation}
 D_{AB}(\vec{r}) = \phi^*_A(\vec{r}) \phi_B(\vec{r}).
\end{equation}
Note that for wavefunctions of a single electron
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{K=1}^{N} D_{IK} D_{KJ} = \phi^*_I \sum_{K} \phi_{K} \phi^*_{K} \phi_J = \tr(\m{D}) D_{IJ},
\end{equation}
however, this relation is not true for matrix densities of multi-electron systems.

To derive the von-Weizs\"{a}cker kinetic energy functional one has to assume that all wavefunctions are real, so that $D_{AB} = \phi_A \phi_B$.
Now consider
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  & \sum_K (\nabla D_{IK}) \cdot (\nabla D_{KJ})
  = \sum_K (\nabla \phi_I \phi_K + \phi_I \nabla \phi_K)\cdot (\nabla \phi_K \phi_J + \phi_K \nabla \phi_J) \\
  =&
  (\sum_K \phi_K \phi_K) \nabla \phi_I \cdot \nabla \phi_J +
  \sum_K (\nabla \phi_K \cdot \nabla \phi_K) \phi_I \phi_J +
  \sum_K (\nabla \phi_I \cdot \nabla \phi_K) \phi_K \phi_J +
  \sum_K \phi_I \phi_K (\nabla \phi_K \cdot  \nabla \phi_J) \\
  =& \tr(\m{D}) (\nabla \phi_I \cdot \nabla \phi_J) + D_{IJ} \sum_{K} (\nabla \phi_K \cdot \nabla \phi_K) + \sum_K (\nabla \phi_I \cdot \nabla \phi_K) D_{KJ}
  + \sum_K D_{IK} (\nabla \phi_K \cdot \nabla \phi_J)
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The kinetic energy density $T_{IJ}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{2} \nabla \phi_I(\vec{r}) \cdot \nabla \phi_J(\vec{r})$ (the integrand in Eqn.~\ref{eqn:kinetic_energy_1e}) appears on the right hand side multiple times. Rewriting the previous equation in terms of $T_{IJ}(\vec{r})$ gives
\begin{equation}
T_{IJ}(\vec{r}) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2} \frac{\sum_K \nabla D_{IK} \cdot \nabla D_{KJ}}{\tr(\m{D})}}_{C_{IJ}(\vec{r})} - \underbrace{\frac{D_{IJ}}{\tr(\m{D})}}_{R_{IJ}(\vec{r})} \sum_K T_{KK}(\vec{r}) - \sum_{K} T_{IK}(\vec{r}) \underbrace{\frac{D_{KJ}}{\tr(\m{D})}}_{R_{KJ}(\vec{r})} - \sum_{K} \underbrace{\frac{D_{IK}}{\tr(\m{D})}}_{R_{IK}(\vec{r})} T_{KJ}(\vec{r}) \label{eqn:kinetic_density_multistate}
\end{equation}
For a single electronic state ($N=1$), we get $C = \frac{1}{2} (\nabla \rho)\cdot (\nabla \rho) / \rho$, $R = 1$ and the above equation reduces to
\begin{equation}
t_W(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{2} \frac{\nabla \rho \cdot \nabla \rho}{\rho} - 3 t_W(\vec{r})
\end{equation}
or
\begin{equation}
t_W(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{8} \frac{\nabla \rho(\vec{r}) \cdot \nabla \rho(\vec{r})}{\rho(\vec{r})}, \label{eqn:von_Weizsaecker_ked}
\end{equation}
which is the kinetic energy density of the familiar von-Weizs\"{a}cker functional \cite{Weizsacker1935},
\begin{equation}
  T_W[\rho] = \int t_W[\rho](\vec{r}) d^3\!r  \label{eqn:von_Weizsaecker_functional}.
\end{equation}
For $N > 1$ Eqn.~\ref{eqn:kinetic_density_multistate} is a system of linear equations,
\begin{equation}
 T_{IJ}(\vec{r}) = C_{IJ} - R_{IJ} \sum_K T_{KK} - \sum_K T_{IK} R_{KJ} - \sum_K R_{IK} T_{KJ}
\end{equation}
that has to be solved for $T_{IJ}(\vec{r})$ at each point $\vec{r}$.
To solve the linear system, $T_{IJ}(\vec{r})$ is treated as a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ and the equation is rewritten as
\begin{equation}
T_{IJ}(\vec{r}) = C_{IJ}(\vec{r}) + \sum_{K,L=1}^{N} \mathcal{K}_{IJ,KL}(\vec{r}) T_{KL}(\vec{r}) \label{eqn:CplusKT}
\end{equation}
This equation has the form $\vec{T} = \vec{C} + \mathcal{K} \vec{T}$, which can be solved for the vector $\vec{T} = (1 - \mathcal{K})^{-1} \vec{C}$.
By writing Eqn.~\ref{eqn:CplusKT} as
\begin{equation}
T_{IJ}(\vec{r}) = C_{IJ}(\vec{r}) + \sum_{K,L} \left\{ - R_{IK}(\vec{r}) \delta_{LJ} - \delta_{IK} R_{LJ}(\vec{r}) - R_{IJ}(\vec{r}) \delta_{KL} \right\} \vec{T}_{KL}(\vec{r}) \label{eqn:vonWZ_matrix_equation}
\end{equation}
we see that the matrix $\mathcal{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{N^2 \times N^2}$ is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{K}_{IJ,KL} = - R_{IK}(\vec{r}) \delta_{LJ} - \delta_{IK} R_{LJ}(\vec{r}) - R_{IJ}(\vec{r}) \delta_{KL}
\end{equation}
Under the assumption $\lvert \mathcal{K} \rvert < 1$, $(1 - \mathcal{K})^{-1}$ can be written as a geometric series
\begin{equation}
 (1 - \mathcal{K})^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \mathcal{K}^{k}
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
\vec{T} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \mathcal{K}^{k} \vec{C} = \vec{C} + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \mathcal{K}^{k} \vec{C} \label{eqn:solution_geometric_series}
\end{equation}
To prove $\lvert \mathcal{K} \rvert < 1$, we have to show that $R_{IJ} < 1$. This follows from
\begin{equation}
D_{IJ}(\vec{r}) = \phi_I(\vec{r}) \phi_J(\vec{r}) \leq \max_{K} \phi_K(\vec{r}) \phi_K(\vec{r}) \leq \sum_K \phi_K(\vec{r}) \phi_K(\vec{r}) = \tr(\m{D})
\end{equation}
Eqn.~\ref{eqn:solution_geometric_series} demonstrates that the kinetic energy density obtained by solving Eqn.~\ref{eqn:vonWZ_matrix_equation} is indeed a functional of the matrix density $\m{D}$. The first term of the series \ref{eqn:solution_geometric_series} is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  (\mathcal{K} \vec{C})_{IJ} &= -\sum_{K} R_{IK} C_{KJ} - \sum_{K} C_{IK} R_{KJ} - R_{IJ} \sum_K C_{KK} \\
   &= - (\m{R} \m{C} + \m{C} \m{R})_{IJ} - R_{IJ} \tr(\m{C}).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Since $\m{R}$ and $\m{C}$ are analytical matrix functionals of $\m{D}$, i.e. $\m{R}[\m{U} \m{D} \m{U}^{-1}] = \m{U} \m{R}[\m{D}] \m{U}^{-1}$ and similarly for $\m{C}$,
we conclude that $\mathcal{K} \vec{C}$ and $\mathcal{K} (\mathcal{K} \vec{C})$ etc. are analytical matrix functionals, which only depend on $\m{D}(\vec{r})$, $\nabla \m{D}(\vec{r})$ and the scalar invariant $\tr(\m{D})$, as well.

Although for $N=1$ it reduces to the simple expression in Eqn.~\ref{eqn:von_Weizsaecker_functional}, the \emph{multistate} von-Weizsaecker functional is an infinite series. 
This suggests that trying to guess the functional form of a matrix density functional from a known ground state functional will in general not be crowned with success.
Taking only the first two terms of the infinite series, the kinetic energy functional reads
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  \m{T}[\m{D}(\vec{r})] =& \int \m{C} ~ d^3\!r  - \int \left(\m{R} \m{C} + \m{C} \m{R} + \tr(\m{C}) \m{R} \right) d^3\!r  + \ldots \\
  =& \int \frac{1}{2} \tr(\m{D})^{-1} (\nabla \m{D} \cdot \nabla \m{D}) ~ d^3\!r \\
  & - \int \frac{1}{2} \tr(\m{D})^{-2} \left[ \m{D} (\nabla \m{D} \cdot \nabla \m{D}) + (\nabla \m{D} \cdot \nabla \m{D}) \m{D} + \tr(\nabla \m{D} \cdot \nabla \m{D}) \m{D} \right] ~ d^3\!r + \ldots .
\end{split}
\end{equation}
In practice $\m{T}[\m{D}(\vec{r})]$ is determined by solving Eqn.~\ref{eqn:CplusKT} for
each grid point and then integrating the kinetic energy density numerically.

\textbf{Multistate von-Weizs\"{a}cker kinetic functional II (vW). \label{sec:vWII}}
For a single electron, the kinetic energy matrix-functional defined by Eqn.~\ref{eqn:solution_geometric_series} gives the exact kinetic energy matrix.
Unfortunately the functional does not perform well for multiple electrons.
So one has to find a different generalization of the von-Weizs\"{a}cker functional to multiple electronic states. A ground state functional can be turned in multistate functional by reusing its functional form and simply replacing occurances of the density $\rho(\vec{r})$ by the matrix density $\m{D}(\vec{r})$. For the von-Weizs\"{a}cker functional this ad-hoc prescription gives,
\begin{equation}
T_W[\m{D}(\vec{r})]_{IJ} = \int d^3\!r ~ \frac{1}{8} \sum_{K} \sum_{L} \nabla D_{IK}(\vec{r}) \left(\m{D}^{-1}(\vec{r}) \right)_{KL} \nabla D_{KL}(\vec{r}) . \label{eqn:von_Weizasecker_many_electrons}
\end{equation}
The matrix inverse is placed symmetrically between the two gradients, which form a scalar product, so that the above equation can also be written as
\begin{equation}
  T_W[\m{D}]_{IJ} = \int~  \frac{1}{8} \sum_{K,L} \left(\m{D}^{-1}\right)_{KL} \nabla D_{IK} \cdot \nabla D_{KL}.
\end{equation}
This kinetic energy functional is not exact, not even for a single electron, but it works much better for many-electron systems than Eqn.~\ref{eqn:solution_geometric_series}.

The rigorous ({\color{red} THERE IS A MISTAKE SOMEWHERE}) derivation of Eqn.~\ref{eqn:von_Weizasecker_many_electrons} goes roughly as follows: One starts with the kinetic energy density, $\frac{1}{2} \langle \nabla I(\vec{r}) \vert \nabla J(\vec{r}) \rangle$, inserts a resolution of identity and collects terms that look like the kinetic energy density. But first some definitions are needed:

Given an $n$-electron state $\vert L \rangle$, an $n-1$ electron state is defined as
\begin{equation}
\vert L^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) \rangle = \int d^3\!r_2 \ldots d^3\!r_n ~ \Psi_{L}(\vec{r};\vec{r}_2,\ldots,\vec{r}_n) \vert \vec{r}_2 \ldots \vec{r}_{n} \rangle,
\end{equation}
where it has to be stressed that the integral is only over $n-1$ electronic degrees of freedom.
$\vert L^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) \rangle$ is not normalized and depends parametrically on the coordinate $\vec{r}$ of one electron. One could also say that the $n$-electron state has been partially transformed into the position representation. With the electronic coordinate $\vec{r} \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$ held fixed $\vert L^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) \rangle$ is a vector in the Hilbert space for $n-1$ electrons, $\mathcal{H}^{(n-1)}(\vec{r})$, which itself is a function of $\vec{r}$, as well. So for each $\vec{r}$ there is a different Hilbert space. The scalar product in $\mathcal{H}^{(n-1)}(\vec{r})$ is an element of the matrix density evaluated at position $\vec{r}$:
\begin{equation}
D_{IJ}(\vec{r}) = \langle I^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) \vert J^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) \rangle
\end{equation}
and the kinetic energy matrix can be written as
\begin{equation}
T_{IJ}(\vec{r}) = \int d^3\!r ~ \langle I^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) \vert \left(-\frac{1}{2} \nabla^2_{\vec{r}} \right) \vert J^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) \rangle
\end{equation}
The derivative $\nabla_{\vec{r}}$ operates on the single electronic coordinate $\vec{r}$, which parameterizes the $n-1$ electronic state vectors.
The resolution of the identity in the Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}^{(n-1)}(\vec{r})$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathbbm{1}^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) = \sum_{K}^{\infty} \sum_{L}^{\infty} \vert K^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) \rangle \left(\m{D}^{-1}(\vec{r}) \right)_{KL} \langle L^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) \vert \label{eqn:eqn:resolution_of_identity}
\end{equation}
Now we select an $N$-dimensional subspace, called $\mathcal{H}^{(n-1)}_N(\vec{r})$, which is spanned by the state vectors $\left\{\vert L^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) \right\}_{L=1,\ldots,N}$. The identity in this subspace is
\begin{equation}
\mathbbm{1}^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) = \sum_{K}^{N} \sum_{L}^{N} \vert K^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) \rangle \left(\m{D}^{-1}(\vec{r}) \right)_{KL} \langle L^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) \vert \label{eqn:resolution_of_identity_subspace}
\end{equation}
However, the gradient $\vert \nabla_{\vec{r}} L^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) \rangle$ of a state vector, $\vert L^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) \rangle \in \mathcal{H}^{(n-1)}_N(\vec{r})$, can have components that lie outside of the Hilbert subspace. The operator in Eqn.~\ref{eqn:resolution_of_identity_subspace} projects the gradient onto its components inside the Hilbert space, while everything outside is lost. This is the reason why the kinetic energy functional of Eqn.~\ref{eqn:von_Weizasecker_many_electrons} is only an approximation.

Note that $\langle I^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) \vert \nabla_{\vec{r}} J^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) \rangle$ is not to be confused with the non-adiabatic coupling vector $\langle I \vert \nabla_{\vec{R}} J \rangle$. In the latter the gradient is with respect to a nuclear coordinate $\vec{R}$ and the integral is over all $n$ electronic degrees of freedom, whereas in the former the gradient is with respect to one electronic coordinate $\vec{r}$ and the integration is over the remaining $n-1$ electronic degrees of freedom. In the remainder of this section the argument $\vec{r}$ and the superscript $n-1$ are suppressed for clarity, so $\langle I \vert \nabla J \rangle$ is understood to mean $\langle I^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) \vert \nabla_{\vec{r}} J^{(n-1)}(\vec{r}) \rangle$, $\int \ldots d^3\!r$ is abbreviated as $\int \ldots$ etc.

The scalar product of a state vector with the gradient of another one defines a vector field
\begin{equation}
\vec{F}(\vec{r}) = \langle I(\vec{r}) \vert \nabla J(\vec{r}) \rangle
\end{equation}
with the divergence
\begin{equation}
\Div \vec{F} = \langle \nabla I \vert \nabla J \rangle + \langle I \vert \nabla^2 J \rangle
\end{equation}
With the help of Gauss's theorem the volume integral over $\Div \vec{F}$ is turned into an integral over the enclosing surface $\partial V$,
\begin{equation}
\int_{V} \Div \vec{F} = \int_{\partial V} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{S}.
\end{equation}
The right hand side vanishes as the volume is enlarged, $V \to \infty$, since the wavefunction decays exponentially at large distance, so that $\vert \vec{F} \vert \xrightarrow[r \to \infty]{} \propto e^{-\gamma r} \xrightarrow[r \to \infty]{} 0$. $\int \Div \vec{F} = 0$ implies that the kinetic energy matrix can be calculated as
\begin{equation}
T_{IJ} = -\frac{1}{2} \int \langle I \vert \nabla^2 J \rangle = \frac{1}{2} \int \langle \nabla I \vert \nabla J \rangle \label{eqn:kinetic_energy_gradgrad}
\end{equation}
Inserting the identity of Eqn.~\ref{eqn:resolution_of_identity_subspace} into the scalar product gives
\begin{equation}
\int \langle \nabla I \vert \nabla J \rangle = \int \sum_K \sum_L \langle \nabla I \vert K \rangle \left( \m{D}^{-1} \right)_{KL} \langle L \vert \nabla K \rangle. \label{eqn:kinetic_energy_density_2}
\end{equation}
{\color{red} ERROR: Eqn.~\ref{eqn:resolution_of_identity_subspace} is an identity in the $(n-1)$-electron subspace, but not in the $n$-electron subspace, therefore the above equality does not hold!}
Using the fact that
\begin{equation}
\nabla D_{IJ}(\vec{r}) = \nabla \langle I \vert J \rangle = \langle \nabla I \vert J \rangle + \langle I \vert \nabla J \rangle
\end{equation}
Eqn.~\ref{eqn:kinetic_energy_density_2} can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  \int \langle \nabla I \vert \nabla J \rangle
  &= \int \sum_K \sum_L
  \left(\nabla D_{IK} - \langle I \vert \nabla K \rangle \right)
  \left( \m{D}^{-1} \right)_{KL}
  \left(\nabla D_{LJ} - \langle \nabla L \vert J \rangle \right) \\
  &= \int \sum_K \sum_L
  \Big\{
  \nabla D_{IK} \left(\m{D}^{-1}\right)_{KL} D_{LJ} \\
  & \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad
  - \langle I \vert \nabla K \rangle \left(\m{D}^{-1}\right)_{KL} \langle L \vert \nabla J \rangle \\
  & \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad
  - \langle \nabla I \vert K \rangle \left(\m{D}^{-1}\right)_{KL} \langle \nabla L \vert J \rangle \\
  & \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad
  - \langle I \vert \nabla K \rangle \left(\m{D}^{-1}\right)_{KL} \langle \nabla L \vert J \rangle
  \Big\}
\end{split} \label{eqn:kinetic_energy_density_3}
\end{equation}
The last three lines are not yet in the right form. To show that each of them is equal to $\langle \nabla I \vert \nabla J \rangle$, one would like to prove that $\langle I \vert \nabla K \rangle = \langle \nabla I \vert K \rangle$, i.e. that the $\nabla$ operator can be moved from the ket to the bra side.

To prove this, the off-diagonal (in the sense that $\vec{r} \neq \vec{r}'$) part of the (transition) density matrix is expressed in the basis of some one-particle orbitals $\phi_{\alpha}(\vec{r})$,
\begin{equation}
D_{IJ}(\vec{r},\vec{r}') = \int \Psi_I^*(\vec{r};\vec{r}_2,\ldots,\vec{r}_n) \Psi_J(\vec{r}';\vec{r}_2,\ldots,\vec{r}_n) d^3\!r_2 \ldots d^3\!r_n = \sum_{\alpha,\beta}^{AO} D_{IJ}^{\alpha\beta} \phi_{\alpha}(\vec{r}) \phi_{\beta}(\vec{r}').
\end{equation}

In the orbital basis the gradient with respect to the ket then becomes
\begin{equation}
\langle I \vert \nabla_{\vec{r}} J \rangle = \nabla_{\vec{r}'} D_{IJ}(\vec{r},\vec{r}') \Big\vert_{\vec{r}=\vec{r}'} = \sum_{\alpha,\beta} D_{IJ}^{\alpha\beta} \phi_{\alpha}(\vec{r}) \nabla \phi_{\beta}(\vec{r}).
\end{equation}
Similarly we get
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  \langle \nabla_{\vec{r}} I \vert J \rangle &= \nabla_{\vec{r}} D_{IJ}(\vec{r},\vec{r}') \Big\vert_{\vec{r}=\vec{r}'} = \sum_{\alpha,\beta} D_{IJ}^{\alpha\beta} (\nabla \phi_{\alpha}(\vec{r})) \phi_{\beta}(\vec{r}) \\
  & \stackrel{
  \begin{smallmatrix}\textrm{rename}\\ \alpha \leftrightarrow \beta \end{smallmatrix}}{=}
  %& \mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0pt]{\mbox{\normalfont\tiny\sffamily rename $\alpha \leftrightarrow \beta$}}}{=}}
  \sum_{\alpha,\beta} D_{IJ}^{\beta\alpha} \phi_{\alpha}(\vec{r}) \nabla \phi_{\beta}(\vec{r})
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Assuming that the transition density matrix is symmetric in the orbital indices,
\begin{equation}
D_{IJ}^{\alpha\beta} = D_{IJ}^{\beta\alpha},
\end{equation}
one finds that
\begin{equation}
\langle I \vert \nabla_{\vec{r}} J \rangle = \langle \nabla_{\vec{r}} I \vert J \rangle,
\end{equation}
as desired.

This result is used to move the $\nabla$ operator between the bra and ket. Then one can recognize a resolution of the identity and simplify Eqn.~\ref{eqn:kinetic_energy_density_3} as
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    \int \langle \nabla I \vert \nabla J \rangle &=
    \int \sum_K \sum_L \nabla D_{IK} \left(\m{D}^{-1}\right)_{KL} \nabla D_{LJ}
    -3 \int \langle \nabla I \vert \underbrace{
      \left(\sum_{K,L} \vert K \rangle \left(\m{D}^{-1}\right)_{KL} \langle L \vert \right)
    }_{\approx \mathbbm{1}} \vert \nabla J \rangle \\
    &= \int \sum_K \sum_L \nabla D_{IK} \left(\m{D}^{-1}\right)_{KL} \nabla D_{LJ}
    - 3 \int \langle \nabla I \vert \nabla J \rangle
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
Bringing the $\int \langle \nabla I \vert \nabla J \rangle$ terms to the left hand side and using the definition of the kinetic energy from Eqn.~\ref{eqn:kinetic_energy_gradgrad}, finally the multi-state generalization of the kinetic von-Weizs\"{a}cker functional results,
\begin{equation}
T_{IJ}[\m{D}(\vec{r})] = \frac{1}{8} \int d^3\!r ~ \sum_{K}^N \sum_{L}^N \nabla D_{IK}(\vec{r}) \left( \m{D}^{-1}(\vec{r}) \right)_{KL} \nabla D_{LJ}(\vec{r}). \label{eqn:vW_ms_extension}
\end{equation}

Figures \ref{fig:vW_kinetic_energy_HMI} and \ref{fig:vW_kinetic_energy_LiH} compare the kinetic energy densities of two von-Weizs\"{a}cker-like functionals with the exact results computed with full configuration interaction as implemented in the PySCF \cite{Sun2020} software package. Clearly \textbf{vW1e} is exact for 1-electron systems but significantly understimates the kinetic energy density for multiple electrons.
The \textbf{vW} functional works well in the vicinity of the nuclei where the electron density is high, but less so in the bonding regions. But both functionals reproduce the transition density accurately.

\textbf{Multistate von-Weizs\"{a}cker kinetic functional III (vW1eII). \label{sec:vW1eII}}
The functional from Eqn.~\ref{eqn:vW_ms_extension} is the obvious extension of the von-Weizs\"{a}cker form to multiple electronic states.
However, it does not give the exact kinetic energy matrix even for a single electron. This can be fixed by using similar approaches as for \textbf{vW1e},
which hopefully also improves the kinetic energy functional for the situation with multiple electrons.
We start with the integrand from Eqn.~\ref{eqn:vW_ms_extension} and write out the gradients of the matrix density
for the case of a single electron, i.e. $\nabla D_{IJ}(\vec{r}) = \nabla (\phi_I(\vec{r}) \phi_J(\vec{r})) = \nabla \phi_I(\vec{r}) \phi_J(\vec{r}) + \phi_I(\vec{r}) \nabla \phi_J(\vec{r})$, which yields
\begin{align}
  \frac{1}{8} & \sum_K \sum_L \nabla D_{IK} D^{-1}_{KL} \nabla D_{LJ} = \frac{1}{8} \sum_K \sum_L \left(\nabla \phi_I \phi_K + \phi_I \nabla \phi_K \right) D^{-1}_{KL} \left(\nabla \phi_L \phi_J + \phi_L \nabla \phi_J \right) \\
  &= \frac{1}{8} \sum_K \sum_L \left( D_{JK} D^{-1}_{KL} \nabla \phi_I \cdot \nabla \phi_L + D_{IJ} D^{-1}_{KL} \nabla \phi_L \cdot \nabla \phi_K + \nabla \phi_I \cdot \nabla \phi_J D^{-1}_{KL} D_{LK} + \nabla \phi_J \cdot \nabla \phi_K D^{-1}_{KL} D_{LI} \right)
\end{align}

On the right hand side one can recognize the exact kinetic energy density for a single electron, $T_{IJ}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{2} \nabla \phi_I(\vec{r}) \cdot \nabla \phi_J(\vec{r})$, while the left hand side, which is the uncorrected kinetic energy density, is abbreviated as $T^{0}_{IJ}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{8} \sum_K \sum_L \nabla D_{IK} D^{-1}_{KL} \nabla D_{LJ}$, so that one gets
\begin{equation}
T^{0}_{IJ}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4} \sum_{L} T_{IL} \sum_K D_{JK} D^{-1}_{KL} + \frac{1}{4} D_{IJ} \sum_{K,L} D^{-1}_{KL} T_{LK} + \frac{1}{4} T_{IJ} \sum_{K,L} D^{-1}_{KL} D_{LK} + \frac{1}{4} \sum_K T_{JK} \sum_L D^{-1}_{KL} D_{LI} \label{eqn:T0_for_one_electron}
\end{equation}
Since the matrix density does not necessarily have full rank, one cannot assume that $\m{D} \m{D}^{-1}$ is the identity matrix. The product of the symmetric, rank-deficient matrix $\m{D}$ with its (pseudo)inverse is abbreviated as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{I}_{IJ}(\vec{r}) = \sum_{K} D^{-1}_{IK}(\vec{r}) D_{KJ}(\vec{r}) = \sum_{K} D_{JK}(\vec{r}) D^{-1}_{KI}(\vec{r}) = I_{JI}(\vec{r}) \label{eqn:local_identity}
\end{equation}
and is called the \emph{local identity}.
The rank of $\m{I}$ depends on the position, for some positions it might have full rank, while for others its is rank deficient. The maximal rank equals the number of electronic states $N$. We call
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{N}(\vec{r}) = \tr(\mathcal{I}(\vec{r})) = \tr(\m{D}(\vec{r}) \m{D}^{-1}(\vec{r})) = \sum_{K,L} D^{-1}_{KL}(\vec{r}) D_{LK}(\vec{r}) \label{eqn:local_number_of_states}
\end{equation}
the \emph{local number of electronic states}, which equals the number of non-zero eigenvalues of $\m{D}(\vec{r})$ at the position $\vec{r}$. Note that for a single electron, $\m{D}$ is the outer product of a vector of the electronic orbitals $\vec{\phi}(\vec{r}) = (\phi_1(\vec{r}), \phi_2(\vec{r}), \ldots, \phi_N(\vec{r}))$ with itself,
\begin{equation}
 \m{D} = \vec{\phi} \vec{\phi}^T.
\end{equation}
This happens to be the form of a projection operator onto the subspace spanned by the single vector $\vec{\phi}$
and therefore has only a single non-zero eigenvalue, which is 1.
That means that for a single electron, the local number of states is always $\mathcal{N}(\vec{r}) = 1$.

Using the definitions for $\m{T}^{0}$, $\mathcal{I}(\vec{r})$ and $\mathcal{N(\vec{r})}$, Eqn.~\ref{eqn:T0_for_one_electron} becomes
\begin{equation}
T^0_{IJ}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4} \sum_K T_{IK} \mathcal{I}_{KJ} + \frac{1}{4} D_{IJ} \sum_{K,L} D^{-1}_{KL} T_{LK} + \frac{1}{4} \mathcal{N} T_{IJ} + \frac{1}{4} \sum_K T_{JK} \mathcal{I}_{KI} \label{eqn:T0_lhs}
\end{equation}

This consistutes a system of linear equations for the exact kinetic energy matrix of the form, $\m{T} = \m{C} + \mathcal{K} \m{T}$, namely
\begin{equation}
 T_{IJ}(\vec{r}) = \underbrace{\frac{4}{\mathcal{N(\vec{r})}} T^{0}_{IJ}(\vec{r})}_{C_{IJ}(\vec{r})} + \sum_{K,L} \underbrace{\left(-\frac{1}{\mathcal{N(\vec{r})}} \right) \left( \mathcal{I}_{KJ}(\vec{r}) \delta_{IL} + D_{IJ}(\vec{r}) D^{-1}_{LK}(\vec{r}) + \mathcal{I}_{KI}(\vec{r}) \delta_{LJ} \right)}_{\mathcal{K}_{IJ,KL}(\vec{r})} T_{KL}(\vec{r})
\end{equation}

Comparing the resulting kinetic energy matrix, $\m{T} = (1 - \mathcal{K})^{-1} \m{C}$, with the exact values for a number of systems with one or more electrons, one finds that ${\m{T}}$ is too small by a factor of exactly $(\mathcal{N}+1)/2$.  The corrected kinetic energy matrix is then
\begin{equation}
 \m{T} = \frac{\mathcal{N}+1}{2} (1 - \mathcal{K})^{-1} \m{C} \label{eqn:von_Weizasecker_many_electrons_corrected}
\end{equation}
For a single electron, since $(\mathcal{N}+1)/2 = 1$ (remember $\mathcal{N}$ is not the number of electrons, but the local number of states), the additional factor does not change anything.
Figure \ref{fig:vW_kinetic_energy_HMI_II} demonstrates that the corrected functional is now exact for 1-electron systems.

\section{Gradient-Augmented Basis Sets}

Typical atom-center basis sets used in quantum chemistry are very far from being complete and spanning $\mathbb{R}^3$. Therefore, if the basis consists of $n_B$ functions,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{B} = \{ \chi_1(\vec{r}), \chi_2(\vec{r}), \ldots \chi_{n_B}(\vec{r}) \},
\end{equation}
there is no guarantee that the gradients of those functions, $\nabla_{\vec{r}} \chi_i(\vec{r})$,
can also be represented accurately in the same basis set $\mathcal{B}$.
For instance, if the basis set for hydrogen has only an s-orbital, $\phi_s(\vec{r}) \propto e^{-\alpha r^2}$, its gradient is a p-orbital, $\nabla \phi_s(\vec{r}) \propto \vec{r} e^{-\alpha r^2}$, which lies outside the vector space spanned by the basis set.
Orbital gradients appear in the kinetic energy density, $\frac{1}{2} \nabla \chi_i(\vec{r}) \cdot \nabla \chi_j(\vec{r})$, so that a large basis set error in those terms affects the accuracy of the kinetic energy functional.

Increasing the basis set until it is complete is not a feasible strategy. Instead one can add only those functions that are needed to span the vector space of the orbital gradients.
In the gradient-augmented basis set the orbital gradients of $\mathcal{B}$ can be represented exactly. In the following, the construction of such a basis set for Gaussian-type orbitals is described.

A normalized, primitive, Cartesian Gaussian (PGF) function centered at the origin is characterized by its exponent $\alpha$ and the powers $\vec{n} = (n_x, n_y, n_z)$ of the Cartesian coordinates $\vec{r}=(x,y,z)$,
\begin{equation}
  \text{PGF}(\alpha,\vec{n}; \vec{r}) =
  N(\alpha,n_x) N(\alpha,n_y) N(\alpha,n_z) x^{n_x} y^{n_y} z^{n_z} e^{-\alpha (x^2+y^2+z^2)},
  \label{eqn:PGF}
\end{equation}
with the normalization constants \cite{Obara1986}
\begin{equation}
N(\alpha,n) = \left(\frac{2 \alpha}{\pi} \right)^{1/4} \left[\frac{(4 \alpha)^n}{(2 n - 1)!!} \right]^{1/2}.
\end{equation}

Taking the derivative of a PGF with respect to one coordinate (let's say $x$) gives
\begin{align}
  \partial_x & \text{PGF}(\alpha,n_x,n_y,n_z; \vec{r}) =
  N(\alpha,n_x) N(\alpha,n_y) N(\alpha,n_z) \left(n_x x^{n_x-1} y^{n_y} z^{n_z} - 2 \alpha x^{n_x+1} y^{n_y} z^{n_z} \right) e^{-\alpha r^2} \\
  &= x_n \frac{N(\alpha,n_x)}{N(\alpha,n_x-1)} \text{PGF}(\alpha,n_x-1,n_y,n_z; \vec{r})
    -2\alpha \frac{N(\alpha,n_x)}{N(\alpha,n_x+1)} \text{PGF}(\alpha,n_x+1,n_y,n_z; \vec{r})
\end{align}

Since the prefactors containing ratios of the normalization constants can be simplified as
\begin{equation}
  n_x \frac{N(\alpha,n_x)}{N(\alpha,n_x-1)} = \frac{2 n_x}{\sqrt{2 n_x-1}} \sqrt{\alpha}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
 -2 \alpha \frac{N(\alpha,n_x)}{N(\alpha,n_x+1)} = -\sqrt{2 n_x + 1} \sqrt{\alpha},
\end{equation}
we can express the derivative of a PGF with angular momentum $L$ as the sum of two PGF's with angular momenta $L-1$ and $L+1$,
\begin{equation}
   \partial_x \text{PGF}(\alpha,n_x,n_y,n_z; \vec{r}) = \frac{2 n_x}{\sqrt{2 n_x-1}} \sqrt{\alpha} ~ \text{PGF}(\alpha,n_x-1,n_y,n_z; \vec{r}) - \sqrt{2 n_x + 1} \sqrt{\alpha} ~ \text{PGF}(\alpha,n_x+1,n_y,n_z; \vec{r}) \label{eqn:gradient_PGF}
\end{equation}

A contraction (CGF) is a linear combination of normalized, primitive Cartesian Gaussian functions\cite{Hehre1969} with coefficients $c_i$ and different exponents $\alpha_i$.
\begin{equation}
  \text{CGF}(\vec{n};\vec{r}) = \sum_i c_i ~ \text{PGF}(\alpha_i,\vec{n};\vec{r})
  \label{eqn:CGF}
\end{equation}

Basis functions with the same angular momentum $L=n_x+n_y+n_z$ are grouped into shells, which share the same radial functions. Therefore the data tabulated in the basis set files consists of the angular momentum $L$ of the shell and the exponents $\alpha_i$ and coefficients $c_i$ in the contraction:
\begin{equation}
L, (\alpha_1, c_1), (\alpha_2, c_2), \ldots
\end{equation}

The gradient of a contraction becomes (using Eqn.~\ref{eqn:gradient_PGF}):
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  \partial_x & \text{CGF}(n_x,n_y,n_z; \vec{r}) = \\
    & \frac{2 n_x}{\sqrt{2 n_x - 1}} \sum_i \underbrace{c_i \sqrt{\alpha_i}}_{c_i'} ~ \text{PGF}(\alpha_i, n_x-1,n_y,n_z; \vec{r}) - \sqrt{2 n_x + 1} \sum_i \underbrace{c_i \sqrt{\alpha_i}}_{c_i'} ~ \text{PGF}(\alpha_i, n_x+1,n_y,n_z; \vec{r})
\end{split}
\end{equation}

The gradients of the PGF in a contraction give rise to two new contractions where the coefficients are scaled by the square-root of the exponent:
\begin{eqnarray}
  \text{CGF} & & \text{CGF'} \\
  L, \{\alpha_i, c_i\}_{i=1,\ldots} & \longrightarrow & L, \{\alpha_i, \sqrt{\alpha_i} c_i\}_{i=1,\ldots}
\end{eqnarray}

To get the gradient-augmented basis, we have to add contractions $\text{CGF'}$ with angular momenta $L-1$ and $L+1$ for each contraction $\text{CGF}$ with angular momentum $L$,

\begin{equation}
  (L, \{\alpha_i, c_i\}_{i=1,\ldots}) \longrightarrow
         (L, \{\alpha_i, c_i\}_{i=1,\ldots})
      \cup (L-1, \{\alpha_i, \sqrt{\alpha_i} c_i\}_{i=1,\ldots})
      \cup (L+1, \{\alpha_i, \sqrt{\alpha_i} c_i\}_{i=1,\ldots})
\end{equation}

The gradient-augmentation is denoted by adding the prefix ``($\nabla$-aug)'' to the name of the original basis set.
A gradient-augmented basis set is at least three times larger than the original basis set. For instance, the 6-31g basis set of neon contains 10 $s$ primitive Gaussians and $4$ p primitives that are contracted into 3 $s$ and 2 $p$ shells, $(10s,4p) \rightarrow [3s, 2p]$, which gives a total of 9 Cartesian basis functions. The corresponding ($\nabla$-aug)-6-31g basis set is made up of the contractions $(14s,14p,4d) \rightarrow [5s,5p,2d]$, amounting to 30 Cartesian basis functions.
However, this is still much smaller than a complete basis set. The resolution of identity
\begin{equation}
\m{I} = \sum_{m,n} \vert m \rangle S^{-1}_{m,n} \langle n \vert,
\end{equation}
where $\zeta_m(\vec{r})$ and $\zeta_n(\vec{r})$ are basis functions from the gradient-augmented basis set, is exact on the vector space spanned by the orbitals $\chi_i(\vec{r})$ and their gradients $\nabla \chi_i(\vec{r})$. This is demonstrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:projected_orbital_gradients}, where the orbital gradients $\partial_z \chi_i(\vec{r})$ are compared with their projections onto the gradient-augmented basis,
\begin{equation}
\partial_z \chi_i(\vec{r})^{\text{proj}} = \sum_{m,n} \langle \vec{r} \vert m \rangle S^{-1}_{m,n} \langle n \vert \partial_z \chi_i \rangle.
\end{equation}

  \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/projected_orbital_gradients.png}
  \caption{\label{fig:projected_orbital_gradients} Gradients of atomic orbitals of the 6-31g basis set of Li in Li$_2$ (solid) and their projections on the gradient-augmented basis set (dashed). The z-component of the orbital gradient, $\partial_z \text{AO}(0,0,z)$ is shown along the z-axis. No wavefuntion amplitude is lost during the projection.}
\end{figure}
